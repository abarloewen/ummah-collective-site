Halal export to the GCC: what a Gulf buyer checks before they reply

A Gulf importer decides whether you are worth a reply in about ten minutes, on a phone, before anyone speaks. These are the four things they check, and the ones that usually fail.

Halal export to the GCC: the short answer

A Gulf importer decides whether you are worth a reply long before you speak to them, and what they check is narrow. Whether your halal certificate comes from a body their own regulator recognises. Whether the plant and product details on your site match that certificate. Whether there is an Arabic page that reads as though a person wrote it. And how fast you answer the first message. Four checks, usually inside ten minutes, usually on a phone.

That matters more than it used to because the buyer does most of the work without you. According to Gartner's 2025 sales survey, 61 percent of B2B buyers now prefer a rep-free buying experience, and Gartner's buying-journey research puts the share of purchase time spent meeting potential suppliers at around 17 percent. The rest is research you are not in the room for. Your website is the part of the sales process that runs unattended, and in halal export it is doing compliance due diligence at least as much as marketing.

The certificate is a gate, and recognition is the lock

Exporters tend to treat the halal certificate as a trust badge. In the Gulf it is a customs document first. Saudi Arabia's Food and Drug Authority requires halal certificates for imported food to be issued by certification bodies recognised by its Saudi Halal Center in the country of origin, and a certificate from an unrecognised body means suspended clearance or a consignment returned to the port of export. The scope has widened as well: since late 2023 the requirement reaches past meat into products containing animal fats, gelatin, collagen, animal-derived rennet and enzymes of animal origin, and into any product carrying a halal logo on its packaging.

The United Arab Emirates arrives at the same place through a different door. The Ministry of Industry and Advanced Technology registers halal certification bodies and publishes the register, and a certificate is accepted on import when the issuing body is registered for the relevant scheme in the country of origin. Recognition lists are reviewed and they change. So the practical rule is unglamorous: confirm your certifier's current standing in each destination market before you quote, not after the container sails.

Provenance is the second question, and most sites cannot answer it

Once the certificate clears, the buyer wants to know what sits behind it. Which plant produced the lot. Whether the certificate covers that plant and that product category, or a different line inside the same group. Where the animal-derived inputs come from, and whether anything in the formulation changed after the certificate was issued. A serious importer is assembling a file they can show their own regulator, and every question your website cannot answer becomes an email they would rather not have to send.

The remedy is documentary rather than creative. Name the certifying body and the scheme in text, not only inside a logo. State the certificate number, the scope and the expiry date, and keep the expiry current, because a lapsed certificate on a live page reads as a lapsed company. List the certified facilities. Publish the product specification, the ingredient declaration and the shelf life as a downloadable sheet for each product. None of that is persuasive writing. It is the difference between a buyer forwarding your page to a colleague and closing the tab.

Halal export to the GCC: the Arabic page and the answer within the hour

Two things separate exporters who get replies from exporters who get silence, and neither is product quality. The first is a real Arabic presence: not a translated brochure or a flag icon that swaps the font, but a language version with its own indexable pages, a mirrored layout and hreflang tags, so an Arabic query in Riyadh or Jeddah can find you at all. Buyers who search in Arabic reach suppliers who publish in Arabic, and in most halal categories the field in Arabic is far thinner than the field in English.

The second is response speed. Gulf procurement runs on WhatsApp and it runs fast. An enquiry that lands at four in the afternoon in Dubai and is answered the next morning from Kuala Lumpur or Hamburg has usually been answered by somebody else in between. Time zones make that structural rather than a question of effort, which is why it is a systems problem: a form that routes to a phone instead of a shared inbox, an automatic reply that sends the specification and the certificate at once, and a first human message offering two specific times in the buyer's own timezone rather than asking when they are free.

The Gulf readiness checklist before you pay for another trade show

  • Certifier recognition confirmed market by market. Check your certification body's current standing with the Saudi Halal Center and with the UAE's Ministry of Industry and Advanced Technology before you quote, and record the date you checked. Recognition is granted per market, never globally.
  • A documents page a buyer can forward. Certificate number, scheme, scope, expiry date, certified facilities, product specifications and ingredient declarations, published as text and downloadable files rather than a photograph of a framed certificate on a wall.
  • An Arabic version with its own URLs. Indexable pages, mirrored layout, hreflang between every language version, and a native reader walking the live site on a phone before launch. A translated PDF is not an Arabic presence.
  • One response path, and a number attached to it. WhatsApp Business as the primary channel, the website form routed into it, and a first-response target measured in minutes. If nobody owns that number after six in the evening Gulf time, decide it deliberately instead of discovering it later.
  • A follow-up system for the trade show you have already paid for. Gulfood and MIHAS produce business cards; the value sits in the seventy-two hours afterwards, not in the booth.

Halal export to the GCC: common questions

  • Is a halal certificate from my home country accepted in Saudi Arabia and the UAE? — Only if the issuing body is currently recognised by the destination regulator: the Saudi Halal Center in Saudi Arabia, and the Ministry of Industry and Advanced Technology in the UAE. Recognition is reviewed periodically, so confirm your certifier's current standing for your product scheme before shipping.
  • Which products need a halal certificate to enter the GCC? — Meat and meat products always. Since the Saudi scope widened in late 2023 the requirement also covers products containing animal fats, gelatin, collagen, animal-derived rennet or enzymes of animal origin, plus any product carrying a halal logo on its packaging.
  • Do I need an Arabic website to export to the Gulf? — You can trade without one, but you will be found less often and trusted more slowly. An Arabic language version with indexable pages and correct hreflang puts you into Arabic search results, where most halal categories are far less contested.
  • How quickly should we reply to a Gulf enquiry? — Within the working hour where possible, same day at worst. These conversations run on WhatsApp, and the supplier who answers first usually sets the terms of the comparison.
